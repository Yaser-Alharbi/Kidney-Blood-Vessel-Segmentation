Stain-robust blood-vessel segmentation in kidney histology

A controlled ablation of stain augmentation and stain normalisation for U-Net segmentation of PAS-stained human kidney, built as a reproducible PyTorch pipeline with a statistics layer and an interactive demo.

1Overview

Stain variation between slides and laboratories is a dominant source of domain shift in histopathology. This project isolates one variable, the treatment of stain colour, and measures its effect on cross-slide vessel segmentation using the HuBMAP Hacking the Human Vasculature dataset [4]. Four otherwise identical training regimes are compared: an RGB-augmentation baseline, HED stain augmentation [1], Macenko normalisation [2], and both combined, as studied on H&E by Tellez et al. [3].

HED augmentation improved test Dice by +0.025 (95% CI [0.009, 0.041], Holm-adjusted p = 0.004). Both Macenko conditions degraded performance, but a post-hoc audit of the normalisation code found an SVD sign ambiguity that corrupts stain-vector estimation on a subset of tiles (Section 3.2). The Macenko results are therefore reported as provisional.

2Pipeline

The system is config-driven: a single YAML file resolves into a frozen Config, and every stage reads from it. Each stage is idempotent and writes to disk, so the sweep can be interrupted and resumed without retraining completed runs.

3Design decisions

3.1 Slide-level splitting

Tiles from the same whole-slide image share stain, scanner and patient, so a tile-level split would let the model score well by recognising the slide. Training and validation each use one expert-annotated slide; the test set is two held-out slides from the second dataset partition, whose labels are model-generated and therefore noisier. The split builder checks the configured test slides against the held-out pool and refuses to run on any overlap, so leakage is a hard failure rather than a silent one.

3.2 Stain transforms in optical-density space

Both stain transforms were implemented from first principles rather than taken from a library, so that their behaviour could be inspected directly and the same stain operation reused in the demo's stress test. RGB is converted to optical density, where stain contributions are additive, and projected onto stain axes with the Ruifrok–Johnston matrix [1]. HED augmentation then perturbs each stain channel with a multiplicative and an additive term drawn from U(−σ, σ), with σ = 0.05. Working in this space means the perturbation mimics a change in stain uptake rather than an arbitrary colour shift.

Macenko normalisation [2] estimates each tile's two stain vectors from the plane of the top two singular vectors of its foreground optical densities, takes the robust angular extremes in that plane, and re-expresses the tile's concentrations against a fixed reference stain matrix. Tiles with too little tissue to estimate stains are passed through unchanged rather than halting a run.

Audit finding

Singular vectors are defined only up to sign, and the sign returned depends on pixel order. Stain angles are measured in that basis, so a sign flip moves the angular distribution across the ±π branch cut and the percentile extremes select the wrong stain directions. This is the likely cause of flipped or rotated copies of the same tile being normalised to different colours. On synthetic two-stain tiles, 52 of 140 flip and rotation variants produced different stain matrices; fixing the sign of each singular vector reduces this to 0.

3.3 Experimental arms

The arms differ only in their colour transforms. Geometric augmentation (flips and 90° rotations), architecture, optimiser and data are shared. Normalisation is applied at evaluation as well as training where the arm requires it, which is what distinguishes the combined arm from the augmentation-only arm.

3.4 Training

A U-Net with an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-34 encoder (segmentation-models-pytorch) is trained on full-resolution 512 × 512 tiles, since downsampling erases the thinnest vessels. Vessels occupy about 4.5% of pixels, so the loss is an equal-weight sum of soft Dice and BCE: the Dice term targets the evaluation metric under heavy class imbalance, and the BCE term stabilises early gradients. Optimisation uses AdamW (lr 10−4, weight decay 10−4) with cosine annealing over at most 30 epochs, early stopping at a patience of 4, and restoration of the best validation state.

Normalised evaluation tiles are cached in memory, so the per-tile SVD runs once rather than every epoch.

3.5 Reproducibility

Each run is seeded end to end, with cuDNN autotuning disabled and deterministic GPU kernels enforced, so a run's outcome is a function of its seed rather than of scheduling noise. The sweep writes its results after every run and skips completed ones on restart, and every number on this page is read from a single committed summary file.

3.6 Statistical analysis

Each arm was trained under 20 seeds, and runs sharing a seed share their initialisation, so comparisons with the baseline are paired at the seed level. The unit of analysis is a run's mean per-tile Dice. Effects are reported as mean paired differences with percentile-bootstrap 95% CIs (10,000 resamples), tested with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Holm-adjusted across the three comparisons. A paired design was chosen because seed-to-seed variation in the baseline (s.d. 0.025) is as large as the effect of interest, so unpaired comparisons of single runs could easily point the wrong way. Additivity is assessed with the interaction contrast ΔHED+Mac − ΔHED − ΔMac.

4Results

HED augmentation. The improvement was consistent across seeds (16 of 20, dz = 0.66) and replicated in direction on the validation slide (+0.018 [0.004, 0.032]), where it did not survive correction (pHolm = 0.066). The seed-to-seed spread on validation also halved (s.d. 0.013 vs 0.028), consistent with stain augmentation stabilising training as well as shifting its mean.

Macenko normalisation. Both normalised arms performed worse than the baseline on test and validation. Two explanations remain open: the reference stain matrix is derived from H&E rather than PAS, and the sign ambiguity in Section 3.2 assigns incorrect stain vectors to a subset of tiles. The second is an implementation defect, so these effects should not be attributed to the method until the arms are rerun with the corrected transform.

Additivity. The interaction contrast was −0.076 [−0.090, −0.063] on test, so the combined arm fell well below the sum of its components, in contrast to the additive gains reported on H&E [3]. Beyond the defect above, the combined arm is the only one evaluated on a colour distribution (normalised) that it never saw during training (jittered, unnormalised).

5Limitations and next steps

  • Training uses a single slide. Stain diversity in training is the quantity under study, so this is the main constraint on generalisation.
  • Test labels are model-generated. Dice on test supports comparisons between arms, not estimates of absolute accuracy.
  • Validation Dice also selects the checkpoint, so validation estimates are optimistically biased.
  • Next: rerun both Macenko arms with the sign fix, add a PAS-fitted reference matrix, and train the combined arm on normalised inputs so that its training and evaluation distributions match.
